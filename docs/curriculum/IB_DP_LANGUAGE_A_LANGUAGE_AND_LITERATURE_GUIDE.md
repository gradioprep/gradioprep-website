## 2. Publicly verified course foundations

The public course overview identifies three areas of exploration:
- **Readers, writers and texts:** relationships between texts, their creators and readers.
- **Time and space:** the ways texts relate to the contexts in which they are produced and received.
- **Intertextuality:** connections and relationships among texts.

It also identifies seven central concepts: identity, culture, communication, creativity, perspective, representation and transformation. The course engages literary and non-literary texts, a range of contexts and forms, and the analysis of how language and choices shape meaning.

Treat these as the course’s public conceptual structure. Our unit sequence is an instructional design decision; the public subject page does not prescribe that all schools teach the three areas in this exact order.

## 3. Learner outcomes for the two-year progression

By the end of the pathway, students should increasingly be able to:
1. Develop a defensible interpretation of literary and non-literary texts with precise textual evidence.
2. Explain how authorial/creator choices—such as language, structure, genre, form, image, register and rhetorical strategy—shape meaning and reader response.
3. Analyse texts in their contexts while avoiding unsupported biographical or cultural assumptions.
4. Compare texts through a meaningful line of argument, not a list of similarities and differences.
5. Adapt an analytical response to a defined task and communicate a coherent argument under timed conditions.
6. Discuss the relationship between global issues, representation, perspective and specific textual choices when preparing the individual oral, using original, permitted learning materials.
7. Reflect, revise and select a next step from criterion/task evidence rather than relying only on a percentage or generic grade.

These are platform outcomes based on public course features, not a verbatim extraction of the full official guide’s objectives or rubric.

## 4. Year 1: foundational analysis and purposeful comparison

**Purpose:** Develop the student’s analytical habits before expecting independent exam-style performance. Start with close reading, make analytical reasoning explicit, and move toward context-aware interpretation and comparison.

### Unit 1 — Read a text as a crafted choice

- **Focus:** How textual choices shape meaning and position the reader.
- **Entry task:** Analyse a short, rights-cleared literary or non-literary text, identify its communicative purpose, and select one precise detail.
- **Model:** Annotate claim → evidence → analysis of choice → effect/meaning. Distinguish observation from interpretation and avoid technique spotting without analysis.
- **Guided practice:** Compare alternative interpretations; improve evidence selection; explain a language/structure/form choice in its actual context.
- **Independent transfer:** Analyse a new text under a short time limit, using an original prompt that tests the same skill but does not copy an IB question.
- **Exit evidence:** A focused analytical paragraph with a defensible claim, relevant evidence and reasoning that explains why a choice matters.
- **Readiness route:** If evidence is weak, return to precise selection; if analysis is descriptive, practise explaining the effect of choices; if argument is unclear, practise a one-sentence line of inquiry.

### Unit 2 — Readers, writers and texts: purpose, audience and context

- **Focus:** How purpose, audience, text type and context interact with textual choices.
- **Teach:** Compare texts that communicate a related concern to different audiences or in different forms. Separate what is in the text from assumptions about its context.
- **Practice:** Annotate structural, lexical, tonal and visual choices; justify interpretations with evidence; write a concise guided-analysis response.
- **Transfer:** Analyse an unfamiliar non-literary text with a question that requires explanation of choices rather than a descriptive summary.
- **Exit evidence:** A coherent analytical line with precise details and justified claims about audience, purpose and meaning.
- **Readiness route:** Move from local detail to whole-text structure and then to the effect of choices across the response.

### Unit 3 — Compare perspectives across texts

- **Focus:** Build a comparative argument between literary and/or non-literary texts without forcing superficial similarities.
- **Teach:** Develop a common line of inquiry; select meaningful points of comparison; compare how choices work in each text; use contrast and similarity to advance the argument.
- **Practice:** Compare two extracts through a shared analytical lens; convert a side-by-side list into linked comparative claims.
- **Transfer:** Produce a short comparative response and reflect on how comparison sharpened the interpretation.
- **Exit evidence:** A clear argument, balanced evidence from both texts, explicit comparative reasoning and a conclusion that follows from the analysis.
- **Readiness route:** If response is two separate mini-essays, practise paired paragraph planning and explicit comparison; if comparison is superficial, choose a more precise common lens.

**Year 1 status:** This progression is a platform design proposal. It does not claim to match a school’s actual taught-work order or to fully map inaccessible official strands. The three-unit count is a starter pathway, not the full IB course.

## 5. Year 2: integration, timed application and assessed-response readiness

Year 2 should deepen and integrate Year 1 skills, not simply repeat close-reading drills. Exact unit order should be adjusted to the learner’s school sequence and confirmed public assessment guidance.

### Unit 1 — Sophisticated analysis under constraints

- **Focus:** Apply close-reading, structure, context and evidence selection independently under timed conditions.
- **Teach:** Plan a brief line of analysis before writing; prioritize high-value choices; distinguish a meaningful interpretation from a list of features.
- **Practice:** Timed analysis of original unseen texts, followed by feedback and a second attempt.
- **Readiness evidence:** The response remains coherent and analytical within the time available, with evidence accurately selected and connected to the task.
- **Caution:** Timings and formats must be verified against the candidate’s current exam session before labelling a task as a mock Paper 1.

### Unit 2 — Comparative argument and Paper 2 readiness

- **Focus:** Develop a sustained comparative argument across studied literary works or the texts permitted by the current assessment.
- **Teach:** Build an argument through integrated comparison; connect authorial choices to interpretation; make explicit, purposeful comparisons.
- **Practice:** Plan multiple potential lines of inquiry; write comparative paragraphs and a timed original essay.
- **Readiness evidence:** Both texts contribute to an evolving argument; comparison is explicit; evidence supports interpretation.
- **Currency check:** Publicly indexed IB Examiner Instructions 2026 identify Paper 2 as a comparative literary essay and require comparative analysis through criterion B2. The independent InThinking page reports the updated criterion applies from May 2026; verify official instructions applicable to the learner’s examination session.

### Unit 3 — Individual oral, global issues and synthesis

- **Focus:** Connect a chosen global issue to precise evidence from a literary work and a body of work or text pairing that matches the current official guide; communicate an organized interpretation orally.
- **Teach:** Narrow a global issue so it can be analysed through the texts; choose evidence for a reason; explain formal/content choices; structure a concise presentation; respond to follow-up questions.
- **Practice:** Rehearse short sections with timing and feedback; refine evidence use and oral organization.
- **Readiness evidence:** The issue is sufficiently specific, text evidence is precise, interpretation explains choices, and oral delivery supports meaning.
- **Currency/format gate:** Confirm the current oral task’s prescribed materials, selection rules, structure and assessment descriptors against current official instructions before implementing a mock.

**Year 2 status:** These are platform design decisions for integrated readiness. The school decides what works were taught and in which order; our course should not assume a canonical reading list or invent required works.

## 8. Test-readiness design

1. **Study:** Explicitly model a response move and why it works.
2. **Practise:** Short, focused skill tasks with feedback.
3. **Transfer:** Apply the skill to an unfamiliar original text or a fresh prompt.
4. **Time:** Gradually introduce realistic timing only after the correct official session structure is checked.
5. **Review:** Use task-specific criteria, give one or two prioritized improvements, and require revision.
6. **Re-test:** A different but comparable item establishes whether the skill transfers.
7. **Audit:** Review whether errors come from comprehension, evidence, analysis, line of argument, time management or task interpretation before assigning the next skill.

Do not distribute protected IB exam text, paywalled model answers or third-party sample solutions in the platform. Link to legitimate sources and author original practice that reflects verified skill demands.
