## Administration and evidence notes

The Listening task is designed around one short, clearly intelligible original spoken stimulus. The script below is an authoring script, **not a recording**. No audio file has been created or selected. Before use, a human performer/producer must create and review the recording for sound quality, pace, intelligibility, age/context suitability and consistent delivery of the marked pause/emphasis. The recording must not rely on an accent as a clue or score accent conformity.

The operational playback policy (one standard play or one permitted replay) remains a pilot decision. The scored task should not display the script/transcript unless an approved access arrangement requires it. If an access arrangement changes the listening construct, record the arrangement and interpret evidence cautiously. Never treat an access barrier as a language-skill deficit.

The Language in Context questions use short project-original microcontexts. They test how language choices affect meaning, clarity and suitability; they are not a decontextualised grammar drill. Typed response guidance is not a maximum and must not block a longer response.

## Listening — 10 minutes

**Stimulus Excerpt ID:** `MYP4-L-EX-001`
**Working title:** A Space Between Lessons
**Script length:** 197 spoken words (delivery cues are not spoken).
**Linked observations:** L01–L05.

### Recording script — performer copy, not a learner transcript

Good afternoon, members of the school council. I am here to ask you to approve a short trial for the space beside the science corridor. Our group first thought the main question would be which books to choose. We soon realised that the more important question was where people could pause without blocking the walkway.

We watched the area during two lunch breaks. Most students passed through quickly, but several sat on the floor near the noticeboard. In a short survey of 24 students who used that area, 15 wanted a place to read, 6 preferred to keep the wall clear, and 3 were undecided. That is useful information, not a vote for everyone.

Our proposal is modest: one narrow shelf beside the existing bench, a sign asking people to keep the walkway open, and a weekly check that the area stays tidy.

[Brief pause; gentle emphasis on “one quiet corner”] We are not trying to turn a corridor into a library. We are trying to make one quiet corner easier to use.

After four weeks, we will ask more students and staff what worked. If the shelf causes a problem, we can move it. A good idea should be tested, not protected from questions.

### Learner questions — draft wording

**L01 — Central message and explicit information**

**L01a.** Which statement best expresses the speaker’s main proposal?

A. Replace the school library with a reading shelf in the science corridor.
B. Approve a short trial of one reading shelf, while keeping the walkway clear and reviewing how it works.
C. Allow students to sit anywhere in the corridor during lessons.
D. Build a permanent reading room before asking students for feedback.

**L01b.** Select **two** details that the speaker gives about the survey.

A. Fifteen of the 24 students surveyed wanted a place to read.
B. Fifteen students wanted a full library room.
C. Six students preferred to keep the wall clear.
D. All 24 students agreed that the shelf should be installed.

**L02 — Inference and speaker viewpoint**

Why does the speaker say the survey is “useful information, not a vote for everyone”? What does this reveal about the speaker’s attitude towards the proposal and the evidence?

**Response guidance:** 25–35 words. **Ideal target:** 30 words.

**L03 — Audience, context and purpose**

Who is the speaker addressing most directly, and what is the main purpose of the speech?

A. Members of the school council; to ask them to approve a limited trial that can be reviewed.
B. Library staff; to report that a permanent reading room has already been completed.
C. Students attending a reading club; to ask them to choose the books for a new library.
D. Maintenance workers; to instruct them to remove the corridor bench.

**L04 — Spoken language choice and delivery**

The speaker pauses briefly before the words “one quiet corner” and gives those words gentle emphasis. How do the pause and emphasis shape the tone or meaning of the proposal?

**Response guidance:** 25–35 words. **Ideal target:** 30 words.

**L05 — Evaluate a conclusion**

A listener concludes: “The speech proves that most students in the school want a reading shelf and that the plan will work for everyone.” Is this conclusion fully supported, partly supported or exaggerated? Use evidence from the speech and identify one limitation.

**Response guidance:** 35–45 words. **Ideal target:** 40 words.

## Language in Context — 10 minutes

The questions below use original microcontexts. Select or edit language in a way that preserves meaning and suits the stated context.

### LC01 — Vocabulary in context

**Excerpt ID:** `MYP4-LC-EX-001`

During the planning meeting, the proposal was described as **modest**: it involved one narrow shelf, not a new room.

In this context, what does **modest** most nearly mean? Select one answer and explain which words in the meeting description support your interpretation.

A. Limited in scale or ambition.
B. Expensive to maintain.
C. Uncertain to succeed.
D. Disrespectful to the people involved.

**Response guidance for the contextual explanation:** 15–25 words. **Ideal target:** 20 words.

### LC02 — Sentence structure and emphasis

**Excerpt ID:** `MYP4-LC-EX-002`

Compare the two versions:

1. The group will review the space after four weeks.
2. After four weeks, the group will review the space.

What is the main effect of placing “After four weeks” at the beginning of Version 2?

A. It brings the timing of the review to the foreground.
B. It changes the review so that it happens before the trial.
C. It suggests that the group will review only the physical space, not feedback.
D. It removes the group as the people responsible for the review.

Explain why moving the time phrase changes what the reader notices first without changing when the review happens. Cite both sentence versions in your reasoning.

**Response guidance for the explanation:** 15–25 words. **Ideal target:** 20 words.

### LC03 — Grammar and usage

**Excerpt ID:** `MYP4-LC-EX-003`

A draft notice contains this sentence:

“The list of suggested books are on the shelf.”

The notice describes the current location of one list. Select the edited sentence that corrects the subject–verb agreement while keeping the present-time meaning.

A. The list of suggested books are on the shelf.
B. The list of suggested books is on the shelf.
C. The lists of suggested books is on the shelf.
D. The list of suggested books be on the shelf.

### LC04 — Punctuation and sentence boundaries

**Excerpt ID:** `MYP4-LC-EX-004`

A writer wants to show that the volunteers checked the shelf’s position after it arrived:

“The shelf had arrived the volunteers checked its position.”

Which edit makes the relationship between the two clauses clear?

A. The shelf had arrived, so the volunteers checked its position.
B. The shelf had arrived, the volunteers checked its position.
C. The shelf had arrived so, the volunteers checked its position.
D. The shelf had arrived the volunteers, checked its position.

### LC05 — Audience, purpose and register

**Excerpt ID:** `MYP4-LC-EX-005`

A hurried note says:

“Don’t leave bags by the stairs. People need to walk through. Move them somewhere else.”

Rewrite the note as a short, respectful notice for students and visitors. Keep the request clear and tell readers where to place their bags.

**Response guidance:** 30–40 words. **Ideal target:** 35 words.
